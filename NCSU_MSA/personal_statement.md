For eleven years at Korean Air, my job was to decide what a seat should cost and who should get it. When I moved to Samsung SDS in 2024, the unit changed from a seat to a shipment, but the question stayed the same: how can an organization make consistent decisions when its systems describe the same reality differently?

**Work experience.** At Korean Air, I operated PROS forecasting and pricing systems and applied logistic regression and random forest models in Python to segment route-level recovery during COVID. I built a SQL-based reservation-misuse monitoring tool that flagged more than 600 cases a year and prevented an estimated $370,000 in revenue leakage. I also led a nine-month effort to merge more than 100 million ticketing and flown records with Delta Air Lines' passenger data, reconciling different definitions of customers, bookings, and routes to enable our transpacific joint venture. As head of the Philippines office, I led a seven-person team across Manila, Cebu, and Guam, built a Southeast Asia dashboard, and trained the team to read the data themselves; bookings grew 340% year over year and reached 80% of 2019 levels.

At Samsung SDS, logistics-volume figures diverged between management reports and system outputs, and some regional offices compiled them by hand. Asked to find the "right" number, I found that six regions and ten organizations were each applying a reasonable but different definition. I led a six-person task force that compared monthly house-bill snapshots in SQL, separated legitimate system behavior from genuine errors, and redesigned four aggregation rules. We recovered 28,306 FEU of misclassified 2025 volume and reduced anomalous records from 0.80% to 0.56% within two months.

**Academic background.** I earned my BBA in Finance, Investment and Banking from the University of Wisconsin–Madison (GPA 3.457), with A grades in Introductory Statistics, Intermediate Business Statistics, and Calculus I. Since then I have kept building my quantitative base through Imperial College London's Mathematics for Machine Learning: Linear Algebra, and the CPIM and PMP certifications. I also built a CPIM study web app with Claude Code, which taught me to review AI-generated code critically rather than accept it.

**Why analytics, and why now.** The task force taught me that trustworthy data is a prerequisite, not a destination. Once the number was right, harder questions surfaced: which lanes need capacity, where demand is shifting, which customers are likely to leave. I can frame those questions from experience, but I have mostly learned analytics by operating systems others designed. To build predictive and prescriptive models myself, and to know when their outputs deserve trust, I need rigorous, structured training.

**Why the IAA.** Three features of the Institute for Advanced Analytics fit what I need.
1. *The practicum.* I want to test new methods on a real sponsor's data, under a deadline, with teammates whose strengths differ from mine. That is where analytics usually succeeds or fails.
2. *The cohort model.* I learn best by teaching. I delivered 74 hours of Data/BI training in Korean and English, and mentoring and revenue-management training brought that above 190 hours. A tight-knit, full-time cohort is where I can both learn from peers and contribute.
3. *Emphasis on communication.* My career has shown that the hardest step is the handoff between a model and the people who must act on it. I want to practice that step, not only the modeling.

**Goals.** After the program, I aim to join a U.S. analytics team working on pricing, capacity, or network decisions in logistics and supply chain, in-house or in consulting. Long term, I want to lead an analytics function between operations and executive leadership, accountable not only for models but for the decisions they inform.

**Contribution.** I would bring thirteen years of decisions made under real constraints across pricing, crisis recovery, cross-border operations, and data governance, plus a habit of making definitions explicit before debating results. In return, I want the IAA to give me the technical depth to turn trustworthy data into better decisions.
