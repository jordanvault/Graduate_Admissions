# Personal Statement (300–500 words) — Draft v5

In an internal meeting during the Group Sales Optimizer (GSO) project at Korean Air, the sales, revenue management, and IT teams sat at the same table. The IT team began describing a system process that no one else in the room could follow, and the conversation stalled. I restated the process in plain terms tied to how group bookings actually work. Sales and revenue management colleagues began asking questions, and the IT engineers, who had been talking past everyone, began answering them directly. Nothing about the system had changed; the people in the room could simply now take part.

That role, translator and communicator, defined my 17 months managing GSO. The project required coordinating Korean Air sales and IT, our U.S. vendor PROS, and Japan Airlines and ANA as reference partners across time zones and languages. For sales colleagues, I described requirements in terms of customers and revenue. For engineers, I expressed the same needs as precise logic and data. For partners, I framed requests in terms of their own systems and vocabulary, using English and Japanese as needed. I came to believe that good teams do not require everyone to think alike; they require someone willing to meet each person where they are.

It was also in this role that I recognized the limits of what experience could teach me. I could bridge teams and build tools, yet I kept reaching questions I could not answer rigorously: why one method suits a problem, how far to trust a forecast, what evidence justifies a decision. After thirteen years of work, I need two things I cannot get on the job: the theory behind the methods I use, and fresh conversations with peers who do not share my assumptions. That is why I am pursuing a graduate degree now.

I am prepared for its demands. I earned CPIM and PMP certifications while working full time. As a parent, I taught myself production and fulfillment to build a product, AiMollaeBook, now past 2,000 units. Earlier, leading a team of seven in Manila, Cebu, and Guam, I learned that local colleagues held knowledge that headquarters' reports lacked, and revising our reporting so their voices reached decisions helped group reservations rise 340% year over year. When I joined Samsung SDS in 2024, I became the newcomer, and asking basic questions helped a six-person task force settle a reporting dispute across ten organizations.

At Cornell, I would bring the translator's habits to a cohort of people with different training, cultures, and strengths: explaining ideas in terms a classmate can use, asking the basic question when others hesitate, and listening before judging. I would share what I know about airline and logistics operations and learn from classmates in finance and marketing. I want every teammate to feel their perspective is needed, because the best decisions come from people who can understand one another.
