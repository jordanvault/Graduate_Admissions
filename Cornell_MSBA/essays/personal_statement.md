# Personal Statement (300–500 words) — Draft v6

I have always been most useful in the space between people who know different things. I feel most at home when I can make what one person knows usable to someone else, and that, more than any single technical skill, is what I enjoy about my work.

I saw it most clearly in a meeting during the Group Sales Optimizer (GSO) project at Korean Air, where the sales, revenue management, and IT teams sat at the same table. The IT team began describing a system process that no one else in the room could follow, and the conversation stalled. I restated the process in plain terms tied to how group bookings actually work. Sales and revenue management colleagues began asking questions, and the IT engineers, who had been talking past everyone, began answering them directly.

That translator role defined my 17 months managing GSO, which required coordinating Korean Air sales and IT, our U.S. vendor PROS, and Japan Airlines and ANA as reference partners across time zones and languages. For sales colleagues, I described requirements in terms of customers and revenue. For engineers, I expressed the same needs as precise logic and data. For partners, I framed requests in terms of their own systems and vocabulary, using English and Japanese as needed. Good teams do not require everyone to think alike; they require someone willing to meet each person where they are.

It was also here that I recognized the limits of experience. I could bridge teams and build tools, yet I kept reaching questions I could not answer rigorously: why one method suits a problem, how far to trust a forecast, what evidence justifies a decision. After thirteen years of work, I need two things I cannot get on the job: the theory behind the methods I use, and fresh conversations with peers who do not share my assumptions. That is why I am pursuing a graduate degree now.

I am prepared for its demands. I earned CPIM and PMP certifications while working full time. As a parent, I taught myself production and fulfillment to build a product, AiMollaeBook, now past 2,000 units. Leading a team of seven in Manila, Cebu, and Guam, I learned that local colleagues held knowledge headquarters' reports lacked, and revising our reporting to carry their voices helped group reservations rise 340% year over year. At Samsung SDS, being the newcomer and asking basic questions helped a six-person task force settle a reporting dispute across ten organizations.

At Cornell, I would bring the translator's habits to a cohort of people with different training, cultures, and strengths: explaining ideas in terms a classmate can use, asking the basic question when others hesitate, and listening before judging. I want every teammate to feel their perspective is needed, because the best decisions come from people who can understand one another.
