# Academic Statement of Purpose (max 500 words) — Draft v3

At Korean Air, an empty seat lost its value the moment an aircraft departed. At Samsung SDS, freight moved through a network of bookings, consolidations, and transport legs. In both settings I faced the same question: how should an organization allocate limited capacity when demand keeps changing? My academic interests center on it, specifically on demand forecasting, optimization, and causal inference applied to capacity and inventory decisions in supply chains.

I approach this question with thirteen years of operating experience and a quantitative foundation from a finance degree at the University of Wisconsin-Madison and an exchange at the National University of Singapore. At Korean Air, I built a reservation misuse monitoring tool in SQL that flagged more than 600 cases annually and helped prevent an estimated $370,000 in revenue leakage. I applied logistic regression and random forests in Python to analyze route-level recovery during COVID-19, and led a seven-person team across Manila, Cebu, and Guam.

At Samsung SDS, I led a six-person task force to resolve freight volume discrepancies of up to 25% between branches and headquarters across six regions and ten organizations. By separating reporting-month shifts from genuine errors, we restored 28,306 forty-foot equivalent units of misclassified 2025 volume, and fixing input requirements at the source reduced the anomalous record rate from 0.80% to 0.56%. Outside work, I earned CPIM and PMP certifications and built and sold a consumer product, AiMollaeBook, now past 2,000 units.

These experiences taught me to understand how data are produced before interpreting them. They also showed me what I still need. Reliable reporting told us what had moved, but not where demand would grow or whether a capacity decision would improve service. I can apply models, yet I lack a rigorous foundation for choosing among them, validating them, and stating how much confidence a prediction deserves. That foundation is what I seek from graduate study.

Cornell's MSBA is the right place to build it. The Operations and Supply Chain Analytics concentration speaks directly to my goal, and the program's emphasis on applying statistical and computational techniques to real problems and real data matches how supply chain decisions arise: a forecast matters only in light of the resources committed in response. Its focus on machine learning and predictive modeling would give me the validation discipline I need.. In project teams, I would contribute operational context from aviation and logistics while having my reasoning challenged by classmates from finance and marketing.

After graduating, I intend to join a U.S. supply chain or logistics analytics team focused on demand forecasting and capacity allocation, taking ownership of problems from analysis through implementation. In the longer term, I aspire to lead an analytics team as an orchestrator who connects domain expertise, people, data, and AI, helping organizations decide which problems to solve and how much evidence a decision requires.
