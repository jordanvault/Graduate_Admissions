**Word count:** 495

For eleven years at Korean Air, my job was to decide what a seat should cost and who should get it. When I moved to Samsung SDS in 2024, the unit changed from a seat to a shipment, but the underlying question stayed the same: how can an organization make consistent decisions when every system describes the same reality differently?

This year I confronted that question directly. Samsung SDS's logistics-volume figures diverged between management reports and system outputs, and some regional offices still compiled them by hand. Asked to find the "right" number, I found that six regions and ten organizations were each applying a reasonable but different definition. I led a six-person task force that reframed the problem from reconciling numbers to standardizing rules. Comparing monthly house-bill snapshots, we separated legitimate system behavior from genuine errors, redesigned four aggregation rules, and recovered 28,306 FEU of misclassified 2025 volume.

That project taught me that trustworthy data is a prerequisite, not a destination. Once the number was right, harder questions surfaced: which lanes need capacity, where demand is shifting, which customers are likely to leave. I can frame those questions from experience. I operated PROS forecasting and pricing systems, applied logistic regression and random forest models in Python to segment route-level recovery, and built a SQL-based monitoring tool that prevented an estimated $370,000 in annual revenue leakage. But I have largely learned analytics by operating systems others designed. To build predictive and prescriptive models myself, and to know when their outputs deserve trust, I need formal training.

Terry's MSBA offers that training. Machine learning and predictive modeling would help me move from rule-based anomaly detection to models that anticipate problems. Causal inference addresses a question I faced repeatedly in revenue management: whether a pricing change caused a demand shift or merely coincided with it. Coursework on AI applications, including large language model tools, would help me judge where generative AI can support analysts and where human oversight must remain. Most importantly, the corporate-partner capstone would let me test these methods on a real business problem with teammates whose strengths differ from mine, then present a recommendation stakeholders can act on. That last step is where analytics usually succeeds or fails.

Terry's placement record also fits my goals. Immediately after graduation, I aim to join a U.S. analytics team in logistics or supply chain, or a consulting practice serving those industries; the program's recruiting relationships with firms such as Deloitte, EY, The Home Depot, and Americold align closely with that path. Long term, I want to lead an analytics function that sits between operations and executive leadership, accountable not only for models but for the decisions they inform.

I would bring thirteen years of decisions made under real constraints, spanning pricing, crisis recovery, cross-border operations, and data governance, along with a habit of making definitions explicit before debating results. In return, I want Terry to give me the technical depth to turn trustworthy data into better decisions.
